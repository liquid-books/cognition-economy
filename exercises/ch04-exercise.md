## Applied Exercise: Build Your First Three Skills

This exercise ends with three skills installed and tested. Not concepts. Not plans. Actual working capabilities you will use starting tomorrow.

You can complete it on any of the three surface types this book uses: a chat assistant, a terminal agent inside an IDE, or an agent-orchestration workspace. The concept is identical on all three — the only things that differ are the container names and the buttons. Current step-by-step paths for each surface are on the companion page at cognitioneconomy.net/ch04-companion.

**Before you begin:**
Spend five minutes writing down your answers to these three questions. Do not skip this — the quality of your skills depends entirely on the quality of your answers.

1. What task do you do most frequently that follows the same basic pattern every time?
2. What task produces the most value for your work when done exceptionally well?
3. What task do you dislike doing, not because it is hard, but because it is tedious?

Those three answers are your first three skills. You will build the first two by *describing* them, and the third by *demonstrating* it.

**Building Skill 1: Your High-Frequency Task (describe it)**

Open your AI assistant. Describe the task as specifically as you can — what triggers it, what you are given as input, what a perfect output looks like. Find your best-ever example of this task and paste it in. Ask the AI to write the skill specification, using the meta-prompt from earlier in the chapter.

Take the output and save it in your tool's skill container — a Project, a Gem, a custom GPT, an agent; the label varies, the four components do not. Name it clearly. Test it on three real examples.

**Building Skill 2: Your High-Value Task (describe it)**

Repeat the process for the task that produces the most value when done exceptionally. This is the skill worth the most investment in getting right. Spend extra time on the instructions. Test it more carefully. Refine it until the output consistently meets your standard for "excellent," not just "acceptable."

**Building Skill 3: Your Tedious Task (demonstrate it)**

This one is often the most satisfying to build — and it is the one you will build the other way. The task you do on autopilot is usually the task you would struggle to describe: it lives in your hands, not your head. So do not describe it. Record it.

If your tool supports recording a demonstration, start a recording and do the task once, narrating as you go, the way you would for a new hire watching over your shoulder. Answer the follow-up questions it asks. Then apply the discipline from this chapter: read the skill it generated, prune anything that doesn't belong, and correct anything it misunderstood.

If your tool does not yet support recording, do the next best thing: perform the task while narrating aloud into a voice memo, transcribe or paste that narration into your assistant, and ask it to turn your play-by-play into a skill specification.

Either way, test the result on a real instance of the task. You may find, after building it, that you feel a quiet and genuine sense of relief. That feeling is the correct reaction.

**Calibrating all three:**

For each skill, run three real examples — not hypotheticals, actual work from this week. After the first run, identify the single biggest gap between the output and your ideal, and add one specific instruction that addresses exactly that gap. "Always open with a one-sentence executive summary before any analysis" is better than "make it clearer." By the third run, the output should consistently meet your standard.

**The Compounding Practice:**

At the end of every week for the next four weeks, do a one-minute review: What task did you do this week that felt repetitive? Could a skill have handled it? If yes, build it before Monday.

Four weeks of this practice and you will have a skill library that is already working for you. A year of this practice and you will look back at how you used to work and wonder how you managed.

The skills are not the destination.

The freed time is.

**Your Submission:** Your submission is the three skills you built — the full text of each skill's instructions, whatever container your tool stores them in — plus one real output each produced on an actual task. Copy the skill instructions into a document, run each skill on something real from this week, and paste the outputs underneath. For the recorded skill, add one line noting something you deleted or corrected when you read what the recorder wrote. Finish with one sentence: what would these three skills save you each week if you used them consistently?

### Reflection

Before closing this chapter, write three sentences:

1. Of your three skills, which one already saved you the most time this week, and how much?
2. Which surface — a chat-based container, a terminal agent, or a background agent manager — felt most natural for the *kind* of work you do? Why?
3. What is the next task on your list that should become a skill before next Monday — and should it be described or demonstrated?
