## Question 1

- A) The invention of the steam engine itself as a piece of machinery
- B) The fact that mechanical muscle became *rentable* — separating physical output from human labor
- C) The discovery that coal could be burned more efficiently than wood
- D) The political decision to build factories in cities rather than villages
- E) The widespread literacy that allowed workers to read operating manuals

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ The chapter explicitly says: "The key insight was not the machine itself." The machine was the *vehicle* of the change, not the change itself.
- ⭐ B) ✅ The chapter states: "The Industrial Revolution changed one thing: it made **mechanical muscle rentable**... you could now **rent power from a machine** instead of hiring it from a person. That one shift — muscle becoming a utility, a rentable commodity — rewrote the map of commerce."
- C) ❌ Fuel efficiency is never named as the key insight. The chapter treats coal as one input to the broader shift, not as the cause of it.
- D) ❌ The chapter discusses factories as one of four leverage points, but never frames urban siting as the key insight of the revolution.
- E) ❌ Literacy is not mentioned in the chapter's account of why the Industrial Revolution reorganized commerce.

## Question 3

The chapter contrasts two mill owners in 1835 Manchester — Thomas and George. What is the central lesson of that story?

- A) Thomas was punished because he refused to adopt the power loom
- B) George won because he hired better weavers than Thomas
- C) Adopting a new machine into an existing operation produces a modest lift; *redesigning the entire operation* around the machine produces multiplicative advantage
- D) Both owners ended up in roughly the same place because the market eventually equalized
- E) The story shows that small-batch craft work always beats industrial scale in the long run

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ Thomas did adopt the loom — he ordered six of them. His failure was in *how* he adopted, not in refusing to adopt.
- B) ❌ The chapter says the opposite: George "hires two engineers instead of thirty weavers." Talent is not what differentiated them.
- ⭐ C) ✅ The chapter states: "By 1845, George's operation produces twelve times what Thomas's does. Thomas is still adding machines to his old structure. George has built a new structure around the machines... the machines become the leverage, but the redesign is the advantage."
- D) ❌ The chapter's numbers make the opposite point: a 12× output gap. The market did not equalize them.
- E) ❌ The chapter argues the reverse — Thomas (the smaller, more traditional operation) is the loser. The chapter is making a case for structural reorganization, not artisanal craft.

</details>

- D) Capital itself is being commoditized; the new bottleneck is regulatory approval
- E) Nothing is genuinely being commoditized — the AI Revolution is hype

<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

## Question 6

The chapter contrasts two consultants, Carlos and Meera. What is the lesson the chapter draws from their different approaches?

- A) Carlos gets better results because he sticks to factual queries
- B) The model is not a search engine — it is a reasoning partner, and reasoning partners respond to *disciplined input* with dramatically better output
- C) Meera is using a more advanced model than Carlos, which explains her better results
- D) Senior consultants always outperform junior consultants regardless of tools
- E) The lesson is that consultants should never bring their own context to AI conversations

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ The chapter says Carlos is "getting value. He is not getting leverage." Factual queries are exactly the low-leverage use the chapter critiques.
- ⭐ B) ✅ The chapter states: "The difference in output quality between Carlos and Meera is not a function of the model. It is a function of what they each bring to the conversation. Carlos brings a query. Meera brings a flashlight... the model is not a search engine. It is a reasoning partner. And reasoning partners respond to *disciplined input* with dramatically better output."

- D) ❌ The chapter does not specify seniority. The variable is the structure of input, not years of experience.
- E) ❌ The chapter argues the *opposite* — Meera's superior results come precisely from spending 20 minutes creating a context file before every engagement.

- B) "We have electricity" — by 1910 this was infrastructure, not edge; AI is following the same arc, compressed from decades into years
- C) "We have a website" — websites have proven to be a permanent competitive advantage
- D) "We have email" — email never became a differentiator and AI won't either
- E) "We have spreadsheets" — spreadsheets are the only true productivity revolution

<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- D) ❌ The email analogy does not appear in the chapter, and the underlying claim here misrepresents the chapter's framing.

- D) Negotiate enterprise pricing with at least three AI vendors before any redesign begins
- E) Move all data to a single cloud provider before attempting any workflow change

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>
