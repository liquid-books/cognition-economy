## Question 1

According to Chapter 15, what is the "knowledge tax"?

- A) A government surcharge on professional services that funds public education
- B) The premium that flows from people who don't know to people who do know, built into every professional services industry
- C) A tariff that the AI industry pays for training data licensing
- D) The legal liability cost that accountants and lawyers must carry as malpractice insurance
- E) A measurable productivity penalty incurred when knowledge workers refuse to adopt AI

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ The knowledge tax is a market phenomenon, not a government tax. The chapter explicitly frames it as "a surcharge that the knowing party collects from the not-knowing party."
- ⭐ B) ✅ The chapter states: "The knowledge tax is the premium that flows from people who don't know to people who do know. For three hundred years, it built every professional services industry in the developed world."
- C) ❌ The chapter does not discuss AI training data licensing. The knowledge tax refers to the historical pricing of human expertise.
- D) ❌ Malpractice insurance is not the knowledge tax. The chapter is explicit that the tax is the premium for the *knowledge asymmetry itself*, not for liability coverage.
- E) ❌ This inverts the concept. The knowledge tax is what professionals *collect*, not what they pay for failing to adopt technology.

</details>

## Question 2

The chapter describes four "moats" that professional knowledge workers have used to charge the knowledge tax. Which TWO statements correctly describe how these moats are being attacked?

- A) The internet primarily broke Moat 4 (Synthesis) and left Moat 1 (Information) intact
- B) The internet broke Moat 1 (Information) but mostly left Moats 2 and 4 intact
- C) AI is attacking Moat 2 (Process) and Moat 4 (Synthesis) — the moats the internet did not touch
- D) AI primarily democratizes information, doing little to threaten process or synthesis
- E) All four moats collapsed simultaneously in 2022 with the release of ChatGPT

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ This inverts the actual sequence. The chapter is explicit that the internet broke the *Information* moat, not Synthesis. "The first moat is already gone... Wikipedia did not kill Britannica because Wikipedia was better. Wikipedia killed Britannica because the knowledge tax on general reference information went to zero."
- ⭐ B) ✅ The chapter states: "The internet (1995–2020) broke Moat 1 completely... Moats 2 and 3 stayed mostly intact through the internet era." Process and Synthesis premiums survived the internet because retrieving information was different from executing procedure or producing judgment.
- ⭐ C) ✅ The chapter is explicit: "AI does not democratize information. The internet already democratized information. What AI democratizes is process and synthesis. Moats 2 and 4. The moats the internet did not touch."
- D) ❌ This is precisely the "incremental" view the chapter argues is wrong. The chapter calls this framing "comforting and also catastrophically incorrect."
- E) ❌ The chapter explicitly shows the moats collapsing on different timelines, not simultaneously. The collapse curve figure shows distinct phases for each moat.

</details>

## Question 3

The chapter opens with a partner at a major law firm and his son looking at a memo the AI drafted in ninety seconds — work that would have billed thousands of dollars. What is the chapter's interpretation of the silence between them?

- A) The father is upset that AI is producing low-quality legal work that his son will have to correct
- B) Each is doing a different calculation on a different timeline — about the value of process work and the future of the associate development path
- C) The son is disappointed his father is not adopting AI fast enough
- D) The silence reflects a generational disagreement about whether AI should be permitted in legal practice
- E) The father is calculating whether to retire early before the firm collapses

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ The chapter is clear the memo had "not a single error the father can find." The silence is not about quality.
- ⭐ B) ✅ The chapter states: "The father is calculating something the son has not yet calculated. The son is calculating something the father stopped calculating thirty years ago. Each is doing the math on a different timeline. Neither math is favorable." The chapter is explicitly about what just happened in that silence — the collapse of the process moat that the son's career was built to climb.
- C) ❌ The text does not portray the son as a critic of his father's AI adoption pace. The silence is descriptive of recognition, not conflict.
- D) ❌ The chapter does not frame the moment as a regulatory or ethical debate. It is structural and economic.
- E) ❌ Early retirement is not mentioned. The father's calculation is about the trajectory of his book of business and his firm, not his personal exit.

</details>

## Question 4

The chapter identifies three new premiums that are replacing the old knowledge-tax premium. Which of the following best describes those three?

- A) Speed, scale, and cost efficiency
- B) Taste, judgment under ambiguity, and accountability
- C) Credentials, licensing, and union membership
- D) AI literacy, prompt engineering, and data analysis
- E) Geographic proximity, language fluency, and academic pedigree

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ These are operational metrics, not premium sources. The chapter argues these are precisely what AI commoditizes — meaning they cannot serve as premium sources in the new economy.
- ⭐ B) ✅ The chapter explicitly identifies: "Three things now command the premium that information, process, and routine synthesis used to command... Premium One — Taste... Premium Two — Judgment Under Ambiguity... Premium Three — Accountability."
- C) ❌ Credentialing relates to Moat 3 (Access), which the chapter argues is "narrower than people think." It is not one of the three new premiums.
- D) ❌ The chapter explicitly rejects "AI literacy" as the new premium — it argues those skills will become table stakes, not differentiators, just as "we have electricity" stopped being a differentiator by 1910.
- E) ❌ None of these appear in the chapter's discussion of new premium sources.

</details>

## Question 5

The chapter describes two traditions of thinking about AI — "incremental" and "foundational." Which TWO statements correctly characterize the chapter's argument about these views?

- A) The incremental view is the conservative, prudent view
- B) The incremental view treats AI as "just a productivity tool" — like Excel or email
- C) The foundational view treats AI as a category shift comparable to steam or electricity
- D) The chapter argues both views have equal merit and the truth is somewhere in between
- E) The chapter argues the foundational view is wrong because it overstates the speed of change

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ The chapter directly rejects this: "It feels conservative. It feels prudent. It feels like the safer bet. It is not." The conservative view, the chapter argues, is actually the foundational view.
- ⭐ B) ✅ The chapter states: "When you describe AI as 'just a productivity tool,' you are not being measured. You are being defensive." It links this to the encyclopedia salesman of 1995 making the same kind of bet.
- ⭐ C) ✅ The chapter is explicit: "The other tradition says AI is foundational — a category shift in what is rentable in an economy, the way steam and electricity were category shifts."
- D) ❌ The chapter explicitly rejects the "both have merit" framing. It states: "they are not symmetrically correct. One tradition... is wrong."
- E) ❌ This inverts the chapter's position. The chapter argues the foundational view is correct precisely because the change is structural, not speed-dependent.

</details>

## Question 6

What is the "Builder's Paradox" described in Chapter 15?

- A) The first to build with AI always loses to the second mover who has better information
- B) The people most threatened by AI are the same people best positioned to use it
- C) Builders are paid less than the people whose work they automate away
- D) The only people who can use AI effectively are those who can also build it
- E) Junior employees build AI workflows better than senior employees can

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ The chapter does not discuss first-mover vs. second-mover dynamics in the context of the Builder's Paradox.
- ⭐ B) ✅ The chapter states: "The people most threatened by AI are, almost without exception, the same people best positioned to use it." It elaborates that the lawyer, the accountant, and the consultant are the ones who know which corners of their work are easy or hard — making them the most valuable builders of the new systems.
- C) ❌ The chapter does not make a claim about compensation differentials between builders and the people they automate.
- D) ❌ The chapter does not require technical building ability. The "builder" in this context is the domain expert who designs the new workflow, not necessarily an engineer.
- E) ❌ The chapter argues nearly the opposite — senior practitioners have the domain depth most useful for building the new systems. The example of "Partner B" tripling his capacity by building a workflow makes this point explicitly.

</details>

## Question 7

The chapter introduces a Role Vulnerability Scorecard with six weighted questions. Which TWO of the following are weighted INVERSELY (where a higher raw score reduces vulnerability)?

- A) Information Dependence (weight 0.10)
- B) Process Dependence (weight 0.25)
- C) Ambiguity Frequency (weight 0.15, inverted)
- D) Routine Synthesis (weight 0.20)
- E) Accountability Concentration (weight 0.15, inverted)

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ Information Dependence is not inverted. The chapter scores it directly: "0 = none, 10 = almost all" — where higher means more vulnerable.
- B) ❌ Process Dependence is not inverted. The chapter is explicit: "0 = none, 10 = almost all" — and process work is the most vulnerable category in the chapter's argument.
- ⭐ C) ✅ The chapter states: "Ambiguity Frequency (weight: 0.15, inverted — higher means lower vulnerability)." Frequent ambiguous calls *protect* a role because they are where the judgment premium lives.
- D) ❌ Routine Synthesis is not inverted. The chapter scores it directly: "0 = none, 10 = almost all" — where high routine synthesis means high vulnerability.
- ⭐ E) ✅ The chapter states: "Accountability Concentration (weight: 0.15, inverted)." Roles where the practitioner's name is on the line are less vulnerable, because accountability is one of the three new premiums.

</details>

## Question 10

The chapter describes a Chicago litigation boutique that restructured its associate development program. Which TWO statements correctly describe what the firm did and why?

- A) The firm increased associate billable hours to 18,000 over four years to maintain training depth
- B) The firm reduced associate process work from ~15,000 hours to ~4,000 hours over four years
- C) The firm replaced the lost process hours with ~11,000 hours of senior-partner exposure and judgment-call shadowing
- D) The firm calls the new approach "AI augmentation" because it pairs each associate with an AI tutor
- E) The firm's managing partner described the bet as cutting the development cycle from seven years to three by eliminating all junior-associate review work

<details>
<summary>Show Answer & Explanations</summary>

- A) ❌ The chapter describes a *reduction* in process hours, not an increase. The number cited is roughly 4,000 hours, not 18,000.
- ⭐ B) ✅ The chapter states: "The new program has them do roughly four thousand hours of those things — entirely as oversight and quality control on AI output." (Down from ~15,000 hours of document review, deposition prep, and brief drafting in the old program.)
- ⭐ C) ✅ The chapter states: "...and roughly eleven thousand hours sitting in on partner-level client conversations, second-chairing high-stakes negotiations, and shadowing the firm's three most senior trial lawyers."
- D) ❌ The chapter does not use the term "AI augmentation" or describe AI tutors. The firm calls its program "taste compression."
- E) ❌ The chapter cites a compression target of "seven years instead of fifteen" — not "three years" — and explicitly retains roughly 4,000 hours of review work (now framed as AI-output QC), so "eliminating all junior-associate review work" is wrong on both the timeline and the workload claim. The managing partner's actual quote is: "We are betting that what made partners great was never the hours of grunt work. It was the exposure to the judgment calls. The grunt work was just the price of admission."

</details>
