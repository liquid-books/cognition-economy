# Chapter 2: Standing Up Your Cognitive Workshop

*Your Cognitive Workshop* — Three tools, one stack, and a setup that makes everything in this book possible.

Before you can do anything interesting with AI, you need a workshop. Not a collection of browser tabs you open when you feel like experimenting — an actual configured environment where your tools are ready, your preferences are saved, and your AI knows who it's working for.

This chapter is about getting there. By the end, you will have three tools installed, configured, and connected to your real work. Every technique, every workflow, and every exercise in the rest of this book assumes you have completed this setup. Do it once. Do it right. Then never think about it again.

Think of it like setting up a physical office. You do not reorganize your desk every morning. You set it up to match the way you think, and then you just work.

## The Three-Tool Stack

:::{figure} ../images/ch02-three-tool-stack.png
:label: fig-ch02-three-tool-stack
:alt: Three-tool AI stack diagram showing Gemini, Claude Desktop, and Google AI Studio
:width: 80%
:align: center

*The Stack* — Each tool has a distinct role. Together they cover every AI use case a business person needs.
:::

The most common mistake professionals make when adopting AI is treating every tool as interchangeable. They open whatever is on their screen, type a question, and get frustrated when the output is mediocre. The problem is almost never the AI. It is the mismatch between the tool and the task.

You need three tools, and each one has a specific job.

**Claude Desktop** is your primary thinking partner. It is where you do your most important work — writing, analysis, strategy, reasoning through hard problems. Claude is exceptionally good at following nuanced instructions, maintaining a consistent voice, and thinking carefully before responding. It also supports MCP (Model Context Protocol), which means in later chapters you will connect it directly to your files, your calendar, your data sources, and more. If you only install one tool from this chapter, make it this one.

**Gemini** is your Google-native second brain. Because it is built into the Google ecosystem, it has native access to your Gmail, Drive, Docs, Calendar, and YouTube. It is excellent for research, summarization, and anything that touches your existing Google data. Gems — Gemini's version of custom AI assistants — let you save specialized configurations for specific recurring tasks.

**Google AI Studio** is your agent sandbox. This is where Google's most powerful models live, where you can experiment with multimodal inputs, build simple automations, and — when you are ready — begin testing agent workflows without writing code. Think of it as your garage lab. You do not do your daily work there, but it is where new things get built and tested.

The three tools complement each other. You will develop an intuition for which one to reach for — usually within a week of having all three running.

## Installing Claude Desktop

Claude Desktop is made by Anthropic. It is a standalone application — not a browser tab — which matters for two reasons: it keeps a persistent system prompt across all your conversations, and it connects to external tools via MCP.

**Step 1:** Go to claude.ai/download and download the version for your operating system (Mac or Windows).

**Step 2:** Install it like any other application. On Mac, drag it to Applications. On Windows, run the installer.

**Step 3:** Open Claude Desktop and sign in or create an Anthropic account. A free account works for setup. If you plan to use Claude heavily — and you will — upgrade to Claude Pro ($20/month). The difference in capability between the free and Pro models is significant.

**Step 4:** Leave the system prompt blank for now. You will fill it in during the Applied Exercise at the end of this chapter.

That is it. Claude Desktop is installed. Do not overthink the configuration at this stage — a blank slate is fine. The system prompt is where the real setup happens, and you will build that properly using AI to write it for you.

## Setting Up Gemini and Creating Your First Gem

Gemini lives at gemini.google.com. Sign in with your Google account — the same one connected to your Gmail and Drive.

**The base Gemini setup** requires nothing beyond signing in. But the real power comes from Gems.

A Gem is a saved AI configuration. You give it a name, write it a set of instructions (a system prompt), and from then on every conversation you open with that Gem starts with those instructions already loaded. The Gem remembers how it is supposed to behave. You do not have to re-explain yourself every session.

:::{figure} ../images/ch02-gemini-gem-setup.png
:label: fig-ch02-gemini-gems
:alt: Step-by-step guide to creating a Gemini Gem
:width: 80%
:align: center

*Creating a Gem* — Four steps to your first specialized AI assistant inside Gemini.
:::

**To create a Gem:**

1. In Gemini, click **Gem Manager** in the left sidebar (or go to gemini.google.com/gems)
2. Click **New Gem**
3. Give your Gem a name and write its instructions
4. Save — the Gem is now available from your sidebar permanently

What should your first Gem do? That is a personal decision — and the Applied Exercise will walk you through making it. But here are the categories that consistently deliver the most value for business professionals:

- **Pre-call briefing** — paste in a name or company, get a structured intelligence brief before any meeting
- **Decision coach** — frame any decision with second-order effects, risks, and a clear recommendation
- **Weekly summary generator** — turn your messy notes and emails into a clean executive summary
- **Email response drafter** — triage incoming messages by urgency and draft context-appropriate replies
- **Competitor watcher** — paste in any competitor content and get a structured analysis of positioning and messaging

The right Gem is the one that saves you time on something you do every single week. Not a tool you use occasionally — something you will reach for on Monday morning without thinking.

## Setting Up Google AI Studio

Google AI Studio lives at aistudio.google.com. Sign in with your Google account.

AI Studio gives you direct access to Gemini's most powerful models — including experimental versions not yet available in the consumer Gemini interface. More importantly, it is where you will run longer context experiments, test multimodal inputs (images, documents, video), and — when you reach the later chapters — prototype agent workflows.

For now, the setup is simply: sign in, confirm you can access the interface, and bookmark it. You do not need to configure anything at this stage. AI Studio is a tool you will grow into as this book progresses.

One thing worth doing immediately: in AI Studio, go to **Settings** and note your API key. You will need it later. Store it somewhere safe — a password manager, a secure note, anywhere that is not a sticky note on your monitor.

While you are in AI Studio, take five minutes to explore the model selector at the top of the interface. You will see options ranging from Gemini Flash (fast, cheap, great for high-volume tasks) to Gemini Pro (more capable, better for complex reasoning) to experimental models that are not yet in any consumer product. This is one of the genuine advantages of AI Studio: you get access to models before they are publicly released, which means you can test capabilities that your competitors do not know exist yet.

The temperature slider — also visible in the interface — controls how creative or predictable the model is. A temperature of zero produces nearly deterministic outputs: ask the same question twice and you get almost the same answer. A temperature of one produces more varied, creative responses. For business analysis and factual work, keep it low. For brainstorming and creative tasks, push it higher. This is a setting worth understanding because it will affect the quality of your outputs in ways that can look like the model being inconsistent when really it is just operating as configured.

## The Persona Question

Before you write your system prompt, you need to answer one question: should your AI have a persona?

This is less philosophical than it sounds. A persona is simply a defined role and communication style. "You are a senior business analyst with 20 years of experience in financial services. You are direct, precise, and always lead with the most important finding." That is a persona. It is not a name and a backstory — it is a set of professional parameters that shapes every response.

The evidence is clear: AI with a defined persona produces better, more consistent output than AI given no context about how it should behave. The reason is the same reason a new consultant does better work when they understand the client's industry and communication style. Context matters.

You do not have to name your AI. But you do need to tell it who it is working for, what that person does, and how they want information delivered. That is the foundation of your system prompt.

## The System Prompt

The system prompt is the most important document you will write for your AI. It is the standing brief — the set of instructions that loads before every conversation. It tells your AI who you are, what you do, how you think, and how you want it to show up.

A strong system prompt covers four things:

**Role** — What is the AI's job in your workflow? Is it a thinking partner, a writing assistant, an analyst, a researcher? Be specific. "You are an AI assistant" tells it nothing. "You are a strategic advisor helping a VP of Sales at a mid-market B2B software company prepare for board presentations and customer conversations" tells it everything.

**Context** — Who are you? What is your industry, your role, your goals, the things you deal with every day? The more context you give, the less explaining you have to do in every conversation.

**Rules** — What does good output look like for you? Do you want bullet points or prose? Do you want the answer first and the reasoning after? Do you want it to push back when it disagrees? These preferences, written down once, eliminate friction from every future interaction.

**Format** — How should responses be structured? Short and punchy, or detailed and thorough? With headers or without? The AI will follow your format preference consistently if you tell it once.

Here is the critical insight that most professionals miss: **you should not write your system prompt yourself.** Use AI to write it. You describe yourself, your work, and your preferences — and Claude or Gemini generates the prompt. This is called meta-prompting, and it produces dramatically better system prompts than anything a person writes from scratch.

## The Three Configuration Files

Think of your cognitive workshop as having three configuration files — the three places where your AI setup lives and persists across sessions.

**File 1: Your Claude System Prompt.** This lives in Claude Desktop under Settings → Custom Instructions. It loads at the start of every conversation. It is your standing brief.

**File 2: Your Gem Instructions.** Each Gem you create in Gemini is its own configuration. The instructions you write for a Gem persist permanently — every conversation with that Gem starts from those instructions automatically.

**File 3: Your AI Studio System Instruction.** In AI Studio, under each project, you can save a system instruction. This is the equivalent of your Claude system prompt but for the Gemini model family.

You do not need to write the same thing three times. Your core identity — who you are, what you do, how you communicate — can be reused across all three. The nuances differ by tool: Claude's prompt can be longer and more detailed; the Gem prompt should be task-specific; the AI Studio instruction can be leaner since you will mostly use it for experimentation.

The habit to build: when you change jobs, start a new project, or significantly shift your focus — update all three files. Your AI setup should always reflect your current reality, not the role you had six months ago.

## Applied Exercise: Build Your Workshop in 90 Minutes

This exercise is not theoretical. By the time you finish it, you will have a working cognitive workshop — three tools installed, configured, and ready for real work.

:label: fig-ch02-checklist
:alt: Cognitive workshop setup checklist for all three tools
:width: 80%
:align: center

*Setup Checklist* — Three tools, three configurations. Complete all of them before moving to Chapter 3.
:::

### Part 1: Install the Tools (20 minutes)

Complete all three installations in sequence:

1. **Claude Desktop** — claude.ai/download → install → sign in → confirm it opens
2. **Gemini** — gemini.google.com → sign in with your Google account → confirm access
3. **Google AI Studio** — aistudio.google.com → sign in → locate and save your API key

Do not skip any of these. Every exercise in this book assumes all three are available.

### Part 2: Meta-Prompt Your System Prompt (30 minutes)

Open Claude Desktop. You are going to use Claude to write the instructions Claude will use to help you. This is meta-prompting — and it is the right way to create a system prompt.

Copy and paste this exactly into Claude:

> *"I want to create a system prompt for you — a standing brief that will load at the start of every conversation. To write it well, I need you to interview me. Ask me up to 8 questions, one at a time, to understand: my professional role and industry, my primary goals and what I'm measured on, the types of tasks I do most often, how I prefer to receive information (detail level, format, tone), what frustrates me about generic AI responses, and anything specific I want you to always or never do. After the interview, write me a complete system prompt I can paste directly into your settings."*

Answer the questions honestly. Do not optimize for sounding impressive — describe your actual day and actual preferences. The more specific you are, the better the prompt Claude generates.

When Claude delivers the system prompt, read it carefully. Edit anything that does not sound right. Then go to Claude Desktop → Settings → Custom Instructions and paste it in.

Every conversation from this point forward starts with that context already loaded.

### Part 3: Create Your First Gem (25 minutes)

Go to gemini.google.com/gems and click **New Gem**. 

First, choose your use case. Look at your calendar and your last two weeks of work. What is the one recurring task — something you do at least three times a week — where you currently feel friction? Where do you start from scratch every time when you should not have to?

That is your first Gem.

Now use Claude to write the Gem's instructions. Open Claude Desktop and say:

> *"I want to create a Gemini Gem that [describe the task]. My role is [your role]. The typical input I will give it is [describe what you paste in]. The output I need is [describe the ideal output — format, length, tone]. Write me the full system prompt for this Gem."*

Take Claude's output, paste it into the Gem's instructions field, name the Gem, and save.

Test it immediately. Give the Gem a real example of the task it is built for. Refine the instructions once if the output needs adjustment.

### Part 4: Verify Google AI Studio (15 minutes)

Go to aistudio.google.com. Create a new project called **"Sandbox."** In the system instruction field, paste a brief version of your system prompt — 3–5 sentences about who you are and what you do.

Upload one document you use regularly — a report, a proposal, a research summary — and ask AI Studio a question about it. Confirm the model can read and reason about your real work.

This is your proof-of-concept that AI Studio can work with your actual materials.

### Reflection

Before you close this chapter, write three sentences — in a note, a doc, anywhere:

1. What is the one recurring task you chose for your first Gem, and why that one?
2. What surprised you most about the system prompt Claude wrote for you?
3. What would have to be true for AI to handle that task completely on your behalf six months from now?

These questions matter. They are the beginning of a strategic relationship with AI — not a novelty you try once and abandon, but a tool you configure, improve, and rely on. The rest of this book builds on what you just set up.

Your workshop is open.
