# Two Ways to Build a Skill — Describe It or Demonstrate It

:::{figure} ../images/ch04-5-infographic.png
:label: fig-ch04-5-infographic
:alt: Two paths to the same skill — describing a task in an interview versus demonstrating it in a recorded, narrated run-through
:width: 80%
:align: center

*Describe or Demonstrate* — Two co-equal paths to the same destination: a skill file you can read, edit, share, and own. *(Figure to be produced.)*
:::

The four-step process earlier in this chapter is one way to build a skill: you *describe* the task, and the AI writes the procedure. For most of AI's short consumer history, it was the only way.

It no longer is.

There is now a second path, and it is worth pausing on — because rival vendors shipped it within months of each other, which is usually the sign that an idea has stopped being a feature and become a category. Anthropic ships it as *Record a Skill*. OpenAI ships the same idea under its own labels. The underlying concept has a name that predates both products: **programming by demonstration**.

Here is how it works. Instead of explaining the task, you *perform* it. You start a recording and do the work the way you always do — clicking through the CRM export, reformatting the spreadsheet, pasting the numbers into the monthly template — and you narrate as you go, the way you would if a new hire were watching over your shoulder. The AI captures the screen, the clicks, and your voice. When you finish, it asks follow-up questions about anything ambiguous — *"You skipped the rows with missing dates. Is that a rule, or a coincidence?"* — and then generates the skill: a written, reviewable, editable procedure it can run again on your behalf. You can share it with a colleague. You can attach it to a scheduled task so it runs without you.

So you now have two co-equal paths to the same destination:

**Describe it** when the task is judgment-heavy and you can explain it. How you assess a management team. What makes a lead qualified. What your standard for an excellent client email actually is. These live in your head as *criteria*, and the meta-prompt interview is the right tool for drawing them out.

**Demonstrate it** when the task is click-heavy and lives in your hands. The month-end report you assemble from four systems. The exact sequence for onboarding a vendor. The formatting ritual nobody ever wrote down. You know these by muscle memory — and you would struggle to write them as instructions, because expertise you perform is much harder to articulate than expertise you explain. So don't write it. Show it.

:::{admonition} The rule of thumb
:class: tip
If you can explain it, **describe it**. If you can only do it, **demonstrate it**. Either way, what comes out is the same thing: a skill file you can read, edit, share, and own.
:::

That last point is the one to hold onto. Both paths produce the same reviewable artifact — and both demand the same discipline. Read what it wrote. Prune what doesn't belong. Test it on three real cases before you trust it. A recorded skill is not magic; it is a first draft written by a very attentive observer, and first drafts get edited.

There is also a deeper reason to care about demonstration, and it points ahead to Chapter 15. Every organization is full of expertise that exists only as performance — the senior person who "just knows" how to do the thing, and has never written it down because writing it down was never worth the time. Recording collapses the cost of capturing that expertise from days of documentation to one narrated run-through. When we talk in Chapter 15 about what happens to professional knowledge when the person holding it walks out the door, remember this section. The record button is how tacit expertise becomes an asset before it becomes a vacancy.
